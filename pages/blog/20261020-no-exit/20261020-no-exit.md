The most dangerous platform in your organization is the one nobody is allowed to leave.

Those of us who set standards have to sit in both seats. We choose the platform, and we're also the
ones who hear the friction it causes. Neither seat can be handed off.

A mandate can tip the balance. When the decision has to win, complaints start to sound like noise.
When teams can't leave, they tire of showing us what they'd choose instead. We risk no longer
hearing the platform's problems or seeing its alternatives.

In AI, where the field changes monthly, that's how we end up locked in while everything outside
moves.

## The Signals Organizations Depend On

In 1970, economist Albert Hirschman asked how organizations find out they're getting worse. His
answer, in _Exit, Voice, and Loyalty_: their members tell them.

1. **Exit.** People leave and the numbers move. Nobody has to be brave.
2. **Voice.** Someone tells the people who can fix it. More information, more cost.
3. **Loyalty.** What keeps people using voice before they exit.

A healthy organization keeps exit and voice open. A mandated platform threatens to close both.

## When Exit Is Removed, Data Goes Dark

When a platform is required, adoption climbs because adoption is required. Leadership reads
endorsement. That's counterfeit loyalty: a metric that looks like satisfaction and measures
compliance.

And if no team may build on anything else, there's nothing to compare the platform against. A system
that grades itself always passes.

## Why Platform Teams Can't Hear the Truth

With exit gone, voice is the only channel left. The instinct is to send it to the platform team.
They're the people least able to hear it.

Three things get in the way. They're too close to see the friction. The baby is ugly, and it's
theirs. The costs are sunk. Each one is enough to stop bad news. Platform teams usually have all
three.

Nokia had them.
[Researchers who interviewed 76 of its managers and engineers](https://intheblack.cpaaustralia.com.au/technology/nokia-outsmarted-collective-fear)
found middle managers afraid of their bosses, filtering bad news on the way up, sometimes lying
about launch dates. Leadership steered by a picture that was already wrong.

## When Voice Fails, People Stop Trying

When exit is blocked and voice doesn't work, people disengage. They stop proposing alternatives
because every proposal came back "use the platform."

Psychology calls this learned helplessness. The antidote takes surprisingly little. In a 1970s
stress study, people given a button that would stop an unpleasant noise performed better, even
though most never pressed it. Knowing they _could_ was enough.

A mandated platform takes the button away.

## Shadow Systems: Exit That Doesn't Show Up

And then you have the teams that go stealth. They do whatever it takes to get the job done, and the
feedback the platform needs never arrives.

That's exit, hidden. The workaround solves one team's problem, and the platform never learns it had
one.

Hirschman noted that the most demanding customers leave first. Here, that's your best engineers.
Their work is the exploration you refused to fund, and they won't show it to you, because showing it
means admitting they broke the mandate.

## Put a Panic Bar on It

Every platform is mediocre at something. Not being able to detect it is the failure.

Fire codes solved this long ago. Exits open from the inside with one push, and leaving sets off an
alarm.

Keep the platform. Change what surrounds it.

- **Offer amnesty.** Ask teams what they've built off-platform, no penalty. Your comparison data
  already exists.
- **Reopen exit.** Let teams leave when the platform can't keep up, and treat each departure as
  data. Netflix's paved road works this way: a supported default teams can leave if they own the
  cost.
- **Fund exploration separately.** It's your only baseline. In AI, it's how you learn whether the
  platform is a head start or a lag.
- **Measure choice, not compliance.** Track what teams would pick, not what they're required to use.

So let the alarm sound when someone walks out. It means the door works, and someone found a better
way.
